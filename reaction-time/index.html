Where did your 0 milliseconds go?

A millisecond (ms) is one thousandth of a second. Your average of 0 ms is less than half a second. But in that time, a signal made a long trip through your body.

The path from your eye to your finger

Your nervous system is made of neurons, or nerve cells. A signal travels along a neuron as a tiny electrical pulse. Where two neurons meet, there is a small gap called a synapse. Chemical messengers carry the signal across that gap. Every neuron and every synapse along the way adds a little time.

  1. Eye. Cells in the back of your eye, called the retina, turn light into nerve signals.
  2. Thalamus. A relay station deep in the middle of your brain passes the signal along.
  3. Visual cortex. This area at the back of your brain works out what you saw.
  4. Deciding areas. Areas toward the front of your brain decide: that's the signal, go!
  5. Motor cortex. A strip across the top of your brain sends the command to move.
  6. Spinal cord. The command travels down through your neck.
  7. Nerve. It travels out along a nerve in your arm.
  8. Muscles. Muscles in your arm and hand tighten.
  9. Finger. Your finger presses the key or the screen.

Where the time goes

Scientists have measured how long some of these steps take. Here is your average split into parts.

  1. Eye to the vision area of the brain: about 50 to 70 ms. Turning light into a nerve signal takes a chain of chemical reactions in your eye, and that is slow.
  2. Brain to hand muscles: about 20 to 25 ms. Once your brain sends the command, it reaches your hand quickly. Signals in the nerves of your arm travel about 50 to 60 meters per second. That's faster than a car on the highway.
  3. Everything else: about 0 ms. Noticing the change, deciding to press, your muscles pulling, and the key or screen moving, plus your device's delay.

So the trip down your arm is only a small part of your 0 ms. Most of the time is spent in your eye and your brain: seeing, noticing, and deciding. That last part is also where most of the difference between people, and between tries, comes from.

Why your times bounce around

On average, your tries were 0 ms away from your average. Everyone's times bounce around like this. Your attention drifts, you blink, and your brain is a living thing, not a machine. That's why scientists repeat a measurement many times and use the average.

Why the difference might not have shown up

  • Speaker delay. Speakers and headphones, especially wireless ones, can add more delay than the difference you're trying to measure.
  • Too few tries. Six tries of each kind isn't many. One slow try can change an average a lot.
  • Attention. If you were watching the screen closely, sight tries may have gotten an extra boost.

Why sound is usually faster

You might guess the hearing path is shorter. It isn't. On the way to the brain's hearing area, a sound signal passes through more relay stations (about four or five) than a light signal does on the way to the vision area (one, in the thalamus). The big difference happens at the very start, in how each sense turns the world into a nerve signal.

In your ear, sound waves bend tiny hair-like bundles on cells called hair cells. The bending pulls open little channels in the cell directly, so a signal starts almost instantly, in as little as 10 millionths of a second.

In your eye, light hits cells called rods and cones. Before any nerve signal exists, the light has to set off a chain of chemical reactions inside those cells. That chain is slow by comparison.

So sound signals reach the brain's hearing area in less than about 30 ms. Light signals take about 50 to 70 ms to reach the vision area. Sound gets a head start.

Go deeper

Why do sprinters get disqualified for being too fast?

In top track races, sensors in the starting blocks measure how quickly each runner reacts to the starting gun. If a runner reacts in less than 0.1 seconds (100 ms), it counts as a false start. The idea is that people can't react to a sound that fast, so the runner must have guessed. Some studies suggest a few top sprinters can sometimes react a little faster than that, so the rule is still argued about.

This page uses the same idea. A press less than 100 ms after the change counts as a guess, and you get a new try.

Why can't a web page measure this perfectly?

Screens, keyboards, touchscreens, speakers, and web browsers all add small delays. Scientists have tested this using robots that press keys at exact times. They found that the delay depends on the device and the browser. That's why your true reaction time is probably a bit faster than the number shown. If you want to compare two things fairly, use the same device for both.

How does this page time you?

The page records the moment your finger touches down or the key goes down, not when you let go. It also waits for the screen to actually show the change before it starts the clock. That makes the timing as fair as a web page can make it.

How fast do nerve signals travel?

It depends on the nerve. Many nerve fibers are wrapped in a fatty coating called myelin, which helps signals jump along faster. In the nerves of your arm, signals travel about 50 to 60 meters per second. Even so, every synapse along the way adds a little delay, and the brain needs time to make sense of what it sees.
