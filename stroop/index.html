What just happened in your brain?

Reading runs on autopilot

For someone who reads a lot, reading is automatic. Automatic means it happens without you deciding to do it. Look at a word, and your brain reads it, whether you want it to or not. Try looking at this word without reading it: pizza. You can't!

Naming a color is different. You don't practice it nearly as much as reading, so it's slower and takes more effort.

Counting is different. Reading a digit is automatic, but counting takes effort, even for small numbers.

Your brain had to referee a fight

On mismatching tries, two answers showed up in your brain at once: the word you read and the color you saw. To answer correctly, your brain had to do two jobs. First, notice that the answers disagreed. Second, push the ink color forward and hold the word back.

On mismatching tries, two answers showed up in your brain at once: the digit you read and the count you made. To answer correctly, your brain had to do two jobs. First, notice that the answers disagreed. Second, push the count forward and hold the digit back.

Researchers link the first job, noticing the conflict, to an area called the anterior cingulate cortex (an-TEER-ee-ur SING-gyoo-lit). It sits deep in the middle of your brain, toward the front. They link the second job, boosting the right answer, to the prefrontal cortex, the part of your brain right behind your forehead. This is one leading idea about how it works, and scientists are still testing it.

That refereeing takes time. It's the extra 0 ms you just measured.For most people it shows up as extra time, even though it didn't show up clearly for you this time. Extra time here doesn't mean anything is wrong. It means your brain was doing extra work.

Why practice matters

You weren't born reading automatically. Years of practice made it that way. Skills that you practice thousands of times start to run on their own. That's useful most of the time, and it's also why they're hard to stop. The other experiments on this site look at what practice does to memory.

Go deeper

Who discovered this?

A psychologist named John Ridley Stroop published this experiment in 1935. In his version, people looked at a sheet printed with 100 color words and named the ink colors out loud, and he timed how long the whole sheet took. Scientists have kept using and studying his test ever since.

Is your brain's control system done growing?

Not yet. The prefrontal cortex, which helps with control like this, keeps developing through the teen years and into early adulthood. That doesn't mean teens can't control themselves. It means this part of the brain is still being shaped by what you practice.

Why the buttons show words

If the buttons were colored squares, you could just match color to color and the word wouldn't get in your way as much. Word buttons make your brain turn the ink color into a word, and that's where reading gets in the way. Scientists choose details like this carefully, because small changes can change the result.

Talking versus pressing buttons

The Stroop effect is usually bigger when people say the color out loud than when they press buttons. That's one reason the warm-up felt harder than the buttons. If you want a bigger effect, try the warm-up again and have a partner time you.
