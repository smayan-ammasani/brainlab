Pattern memory

Why do experts remember so much?

What you'll do

A grid of squares will appear with some squares filled in. You get 5 seconds to look. Then the grid clears, and you rebuild it by tapping the squares that were filled. Press Check to see how you did.

You'll do 2 practice grids, then 8 real ones.

  • About 5 minutes
  • Works with sound off
  • Mouse, touchscreen, or keyboard

This test needs you to see the grid on the screen. With a keyboard, use the arrow keys to move around the grid and Space to fill or clear a square.

What just happened in your brain?

Same squares, different chunks

Each shape grid had the same number of filled squares as its random partner. So why was one easier? When you saw a shape, you didn't have to remember a dozen separate squares. You remembered one or two chunks: "a big letter T, starting at the top." Your long-term memory already knew what a T looks like, so your small working memory only had to hold a little.

The random grids had no chunks you already knew. Each square was its own thing to remember, and working memory fills up fast.

The chess experiment

In the early 1970s, two scientists named William Chase and Herbert Simon tried this with chess. They showed chess players a board for about five seconds, then asked them to set up the pieces from memory. A master rebuilt positions from real games far better than a beginner did.

Then they scrambled the pieces into random spots. The master's advantage mostly disappeared. Later research found that strong players still keep a small edge on random boards, but it's much smaller.

Experts have better chunks, not bigger memories

The masters had not grown bigger working memories. Years of playing had given them a huge library of familiar chunks, like "castled king with three pawns in front." Researchers estimate that chess masters have tens of thousands of chunks stored in long-term memory. On a real board, they see a few big chunks. On a random board, there are no chunks to find, so they are stuck with the same small workspace as everyone else.

The same idea shows up in music, sports, computer programming, and many other fields: experts remember things from their own field far better, mostly because of what they already know. It even ties back to the Stroop test. Reading is something you're an expert at, which is why a word jumps out at you before you can stop it.

Where this leads

Researchers now study expertise by timing decisions, not just by testing memory. For example, one team looked at millions of online chess games and timed how long players thought before each move. Players tended to think longer when extra thinking would pay off, and stronger players did this better. A quick move can be a sign that a player recognized something familiar.

Go deeper

How many chunks does an expert know?

Nobody can count them directly. But computer models of chess memory suggest that masters store something like tens of thousands of familiar patterns. Building that library takes years of practice and study.

Why didn't the masters' advantage disappear completely?

Even a scrambled board has a few pieces that happen to land in familiar spots, like two pawns side by side. Experts can grab those little chunks. So the advantage gets much smaller, but not quite zero. The same thing happens with experts in other fields.

Why the grids were matched

If the shape grids had fewer filled squares than the random ones, they would be easier for a boring reason. Matching the number of squares means the only real difference is whether the squares make a shape you know. That's what makes it a fair test.
