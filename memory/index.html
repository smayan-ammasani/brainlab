What just happened in your brain?

Working memory is a small workspace

Working memory is your mind's small workspace. It holds what you're using right now, like a number you're about to type or the start of a sentence you're reading. It is different from long-term memory, your huge store of things you know, like your birthday or how to ride a bike.

How big is the workspace?

In 1956, a psychologist named George Miller described a limit of about seven items. Later research suggests the real limit is smaller: about three to five chunks, often summed up as "about four," when people can't repeat things to themselves or group them. Digit spans usually come out bigger than four because people silently repeat the digits and find groups.

Chunking: same slots, bigger loads

A chunk is a group you already know as one thing. When you saw 2 4 6 8, you didn't have to hold four digits. You held one thing: counting by twos. The number of slots in your workspace didn't change. What changed is how much each slot held.

Where did the chunks come from? From long-term memory. You already knew 2468, 365, and 1000 before you started. Your long-term memory helped your working memory do more.

Here's the big idea: experts don't have bigger working memories. They have better chunks. The next experiment tests that.

Go deeper

Seven or four?

George Miller's famous 1956 paper was called "The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two." Seven is close to how many digits many people can repeat back. But people repeat digits in their heads and group them, which stretches the number. When scientists block those tricks, the limit drops to about three to five chunks. So both numbers are right, about different things.

Why did the strings alternate?

The random and patterned strings took turns. If all the random ones came first, you'd get extra practice before the patterned ones, and that practice could make the patterned span look better for the wrong reason. Taking turns makes it a fairer test. Scientists call this kind of problem a confound.

Does this measure how smart you are?

No. Your span changes with sleep, focus, practice, and strategies like chunking. It shows how one part of your memory works. It isn't a score for your brain.

Use chunking on purpose

Next time you need to remember a long number, look for groups you already know: a year, a countdown, a double. You just saw how much that can help.
