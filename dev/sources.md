# Sources

Every factual claim on the site traces to an entry here. Each entry gives the claim as the site states it, where it appears, the source, and its status.

Status key: **Confirmed** (checked against the source), **Corrected** (the original plan or spec was wrong and the site text was fixed), **[VERIFY]** (could not confirm; the site avoids relying on it or flags it).

Checked: 2026-10-15 unless noted.

## Reaction time (`reaction-time/`)

### RT-1. Typical simple reaction times to a light
- **Site says:** Studies that use ordinary computers have measured average reaction times to a light from about 230 ms to almost 400 ms. Careful lab setups that correct for equipment delay find a little over 200 ms for young adults.
- **Source:** Woods, D. L., et al. (2015). Factors influencing the latency of simple reaction time. *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience*, 9, 131. https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2015.00131 (open access). Abstract: mean SRT 231 ms, 213 ms when corrected for hardware delays. Introduction and Table 1: recent computer-based means range from 233 ms to nearly 400 ms.
- **Status:** Confirmed. Framed as a fact about measurement and devices, never as a norm for the student.

### RT-2. Light reaches the visual cortex in about 50 to 70 ms
- **Site says:** Signals from the eye reach the vision area at the back of the brain about 50 to 70 ms after the light appears.
- **Sources:**
  - Vanni, S., et al. (2001). Coinciding early activation of the human primary visual cortex and anteromedial cuneus. *PNAS*, 98(5), 2776-2780. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.041600898 (open access, PMC30215). Activation emerged 55 to 70 ms after stimulus onset in primary visual areas.
  - Foxe, J. J., & Simpson, G. V. (2002). Flow of activation from V1 to frontal cortex in humans. *Experimental Brain Research*, 142, 139-150. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00221-001-0906-7. Mean onset over occipital cortex 56 ms; dorsolateral frontal cortex active by about 80 ms.
- **Status:** Confirmed.

### RT-3. Motor cortex to hand muscle in about 20 to 25 ms
- **Site says:** Once the movement area of the brain sends the command, the signal reaches the muscles of the hand in about 20 to 25 ms.
- **Sources:** Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) studies measure the motor evoked potential latency from motor cortex to a hand muscle.
  - Normal parameters for diagnostic transcranial magnetic stimulation using a parabolic coil with biphasic pulse stimulation. *BMC Neurology* (2022). https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9805266/ (open access). Abductor pollicis brevis latencies about 21 to 22 ms in 48 healthy adults.
  - Cantone, M., et al. (2023). Sex-specific reference values for total, central, and peripheral latency of motor evoked potentials from a large cohort. *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience*, 17, 1152204. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10288153/
- **Status:** Confirmed (about 20 to 23 ms in adults; the site says "about 20 to 25 ms" to allow for a resting hand and for younger people, whose latencies are a little longer).

### RT-4. Nerve signals in the arm travel about 50 to 60 meters per second
- **Site says:** Signals in the nerves of your arm travel about 50 to 60 meters per second.
- **Sources:** Normative nerve conduction studies of the median nerve in the forearm report means of about 55 to 58 m/s with lower limits near 50 m/s. Example: Czech multicenter normative study (2014), *Ceska a Slovenska Neurologie a Neurochirurgie*, forearm median motor conduction velocity 57.1 plus or minus 4.6 m/s (https://www.csnn.eu/pdf?id=48194). Also an Oslo normative dataset, ages 10 to 19, mean 55.8 m/s.
- **Status:** Confirmed. Conversion: 60 m/s is about 216 km/h, about 134 mph.

### RT-5. Neurons, synapses, and electrical signals
- **Site says:** A neuron is a nerve cell. Nerve signals travel along neurons as electrical impulses. At a synapse, the gap between two neurons, the signal is passed on by chemical messengers. Many nerve fibers are wrapped in a fatty coating called myelin, which helps signals travel faster.
- **Source:** Society for Neuroscience, *Brain Facts* (8th ed.), Chapter 1, "Brain Basics." https://www.brainfacts.org/the-brain-facts-book
- **Status:** Confirmed (textbook fact).

### RT-6. The visual pathway: eye, thalamus, visual cortex at the back of the brain
- **Site says:** The retina turns light into nerve signals. They travel to the thalamus, a relay station deep in the brain, then to the visual cortex at the back of the brain. The motor cortex, a strip across the top of the brain, sends movement commands down the spinal cord and out along nerves to muscles.
- **Sources:** *Brain Facts*, chapters on vision and movement (https://www.brainfacts.org/the-brain-facts-book). Open textbook: *Introduction to Neuroscience* (Michigan State University Libraries), https://openbooks.lib.msu.edu/introneuroscience1/
- **Status:** Confirmed (textbook fact). The diagram is labeled "simplified, not to scale."

### RT-7. The 100 ms false-start rule in sprinting
- **Site says:** In top sprint races, sensors in the starting blocks flag a false start if a runner reacts less than 0.1 seconds (100 ms) after the gun, because people are not thought to react to a sound that fast.
- **Sources:** World Athletics Technical Rules, TR 16.6 (2020 edition) as quoted by FinishLynx false-start equipment documentation: https://www.finishlynx.com/product/reactime-false-start-detection/ . Overview: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/False_start . 2022 example (Devon Allen, 0.099 s), AP explainer: https://www.news4jax.com/sports/2022/07/18/explainer-was-devon-allen-too-fast-for-his-own-good/
- **Also says:** Some studies suggest a few top sprinters can sometimes react a little faster than 0.1 s, so the rule is still argued about. Source: Wikipedia "False start" summarizes an IAAF-commissioned study (2009) indicating top sprinters can sometimes react in about 0.08 s; the 2022 Devon Allen case (0.099 s) drew public debate (AP explainer above).
- **Status:** Confirmed. The site does not claim the rule is beyond debate.

### RT-8. Web pages, screens, keyboards, and speakers add delay
- **Site says:** Your device adds some delay that this page cannot measure, so your true reaction time is probably a little faster than the number shown. Scientists have tested this and found that the amount depends on the device and browser.
- **Sources:**
  - Bridges, D., Pitiot, A., MacAskill, M. R., & Peirce, J. W. (2020). The timing mega-study: comparing a range of experiment generators, both lab-based and online. *PeerJ*, 8, e9414. https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.9414 (open access).
  - Anwyl-Irvine, A., Dalmaijer, E. S., Hodges, N., & Evershed, J. K. (2021). Realistic precision and accuracy of online experiment platforms, web browsers, and devices. *Behavior Research Methods*, 53, 1407-1425. https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-020-01501-5 (open access, PMC8367876).
- **Status:** Confirmed.

### RT-9. Why hearing is fast: hair cells open channels directly; vision uses a slower chemical chain
- **Site says:** In the ear, sound bends tiny bundles on hair cells, which pulls open channels directly and starts a signal almost instantly. In the eye, light must set off a chain of chemical reactions inside rods and cones before any signal exists, which is slow by comparison.
- **Source:** Purves, D., et al. (eds.) (2001). *Neuroscience*, 2nd ed. "Hair Cells and the Mechanoelectrical Transduction of Sound Waves." NCBI Bookshelf. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK10867/ . Hair cells convert bundle movement into an electrical signal in as little as 10 microseconds; the need for speed rules out "the relatively slow second messenger pathways used in visual and olfactory transduction"; tip links directly open channels.
- **Status:** Confirmed. This replaces the original plan's "the auditory pathway is shorter" (SPEC section 14, item 2).

### RT-10. Hearing reaches its cortex sooner than vision reaches its cortex
- **Site says:** Sound signals reach the hearing area of the cortex in less than about 30 ms. Light signals take about 50 to 70 ms to reach the vision area.
- **Sources:**
  - Trébuchon, A., Alario, F.-X., & Liégeois-Chauvel, C. (2021). Functional topography of auditory areas derived from the combination of electrophysiological recordings and cortical electrical stimulation. *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience*, 15, 702773. https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2021.702773 (open access). Primary auditory components have latencies below 30 ms in Heschl's gyrus.
  - Vision: see RT-2.
- **Status:** Confirmed for "less than about 30 ms." A first volley near 13 ms in Heschl's gyrus is reported secondhand (Liégeois-Chauvel's intracranial work, summarized by D. L. Woods on the Auditory list, 1999); the site does not use that single number.

### RT-11. The hearing pathway has more relay stations than the vision pathway
- **Site says:** The hearing pathway actually passes through more relay stations on the way to the cortex (about four or five) than the vision pathway (one, in the thalamus, after the retina).
- **Sources:** *Introduction to Neuroscience* (MSU open textbook), "Central Auditory Processing": cochlear nucleus, superior olivary complex, lateral lemniscus, inferior colliculus, medial geniculate nucleus of the thalamus, then auditory cortex. https://openbooks.lib.msu.edu/introneuroscience1/chapter/central-auditory-processing/ . Visual pathway: retina, lateral geniculate nucleus of the thalamus, primary visual cortex (same textbook, vision chapters; *Brain Facts* vision chapter).
- **Status:** Confirmed. Wording kept approximate ("about four or five") because the lateral lemniscus nuclei are sometimes counted separately.

### RT-12. Reaction to sound is usually faster than to light
- **Site says (skip path, labeled as typical, not the student's):** In one small study of 14 adults on a laptop, the average reaction time was about 331 ms to a light and about 284 ms to a sound (Shelton and Kumar, 2010). A larger study of 120 students also found sound reactions faster than light reactions.
- **Sources:**
  - Shelton, J., & Kumar, G. P. (2010). Comparison between auditory and visual simple reaction times. *Neuroscience & Medicine*, 1(1), 30-32. https://www.scirp.org/html/4-2400003_2689.htm (open access).
  - Jain, A., Bansal, R., Kumar, A., & Singh, K. D. (2015). A comparative study of visual and auditory reaction times on the basis of gender and physical activity levels of medical first year students. *International Journal of Applied and Basic Medical Research*, 5(2), 124-127. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4456887/
- **Status:** Confirmed.

### RT-13. Bluetooth audio delay
- **Site says:** Wireless (Bluetooth) headphones and speakers often add a delay of around 100 to 300 ms, which is bigger than the difference this round is trying to measure.
- **Sources:** RTINGS.com, "Headphones latency" (measured SBC 173 ms, aptX 166 ms, aptX Low Latency 34 ms): https://www.rtings.com/headphones/learn/latency . SoundStage (2014): SBC typical latency 200 to 250 ms. General overview listing 30 to 300 ms depending on codec and device.
- **Status:** Confirmed as an approximate range; varies a lot by device. The site says "often" and "around."

### RT-14. PhET Neuron simulation
- **Site says:** PhET's Neuron simulation (University of Colorado Boulder) shows how a single neuron fires. It runs in the browser (HTML5) and works on Chromebooks.
- **Source:** https://phet.colorado.edu/en/simulations/neuron . PhET search lists "Neuron (HTML5)."
- **Status:** Confirmed. Recheck the link before launch.

## Stroop test (`stroop/`)

### S-1. Stroop's 1935 study
- **Site says:** John Ridley Stroop published this effect in 1935.
- **Source:** Stroop, J. R. (1935). Studies of interference in serial verbal reactions. *Journal of Experimental Psychology*, 18(6), 643-662. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0054651 . Full text: https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Stroop/
- **Status:** Confirmed.

### S-2. Conflict detection and control in the brain
- **Site says:** One leading idea: a region called the anterior cingulate cortex helps notice the conflict, and the prefrontal cortex at the front of the brain boosts the ink color over the word.
- **Sources:**
  - Botvinick, M. M., Braver, T. S., Barch, D. M., Carter, C. S., & Cohen, J. D. (2001). Conflict monitoring and cognitive control. *Psychological Review*, 108(3), 624-652. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.108.3.624
  - MacDonald, A. W., Cohen, J. D., Stenger, V. A., & Carter, C. S. (2000). Dissociating the role of the dorsolateral prefrontal and anterior cingulate cortex in cognitive control. *Science*, 288, 1835-1838. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.288.5472.1835
  - Carter, C. S., & van Veen, V. (2007). Anterior cingulate cortex and conflict detection: an update of theory and data. *Cognitive, Affective, & Behavioral Neuroscience*, 7(4), 367-379. https://doi.org/10.3758/CABN.7.4.367
- **Status:** Confirmed as a leading model. The site says "researchers link" and "one leading idea," not "this is exactly how it works," because the model is debated.

### S-3. Control systems keep developing through the teen years
- **Site says:** The front of the brain, which helps with control like this, keeps developing through the teen years and into early adulthood.
- **Source:** BrainFacts.org, "Ask a Neuroscientist: Children's brains." https://www.brainfacts.org/ask-an-expert/childrens-brains . The prefrontal cortex "undergoes particularly protracted development during the teenage years and beyond"; prefrontal gray matter changes continue through the 20s.
- **Status:** Confirmed. The site avoids the oversimplified "finishes at 25" claim.

### S-4. The counting (numbers) version
- **Site says:** Counting digits is slower when the digits disagree with how many there are, because reading a digit is automatic and counting takes effort.
- **Sources:**
  - Muroi, M., & MacLeod, C. M. (2004). Can the Stroop effect be reduced by spatially separating the conflicting dimensions? *Japanese Psychological Research*, 46(4). https://uwaterloo.ca/memory-attention-cognition-lab/sites/default/files/uploads/files/jpr04.pdf . Counting digits is impaired when the digits are incompatible with their numerosity.
  - Overview: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_Stroop_effect
- **Status:** Confirmed.

### S-5. Button presses show a smaller effect than speaking
- **Site says:** The Stroop effect is usually bigger when people say the colors out loud than when they press buttons, which is one reason your number may be small.
- **Source:** Augustinova, M., Parris, B. A., & Ferrand, L. (2019). The loci of Stroop interference and facilitation effects with manual and vocal responses. *Frontiers in Psychology*, 10, 1786. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.01786 (open access). Interference "substantially larger" with vocal responses.
- **Status:** Confirmed.

### S-6. Ink colors and contrast
- **Choice:** Four colors from the Okabe-Ito color-universal-design palette, which was designed to stay distinguishable for the common types of color blindness: orange #E69F00, sky blue #56B4E9, bluish green #009E73, reddish purple (called "pink" on the site) #CC79A7. Shown as large bold text on the dark stimulus panel. Red and green are not both used.
- **Source:** Okabe, M., & Ito, K. (2008). Color Universal Design. https://jfly.uni-koeln.de/color/ (hex values confirmed by multiple secondary references).
- **Contrast ratios:** see "Stroop colors" under Design checks below.
- **Status:** Confirmed.

### S-7. Mistakes are also a sign of interference
- **Site says:** More mistakes when the word and ink disagree is another sign of the fight: sometimes the word wins.
- **Source:** MacLeod, C. M. (1991). Half a century of research on the Stroop effect: An integrative review. *Psychological Bulletin*, 109(2), 163-203. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.109.2.163 . Interference appears in errors as well as in response times.
- **Status:** Confirmed (well-established; shown only when the student's own data has more mismatching mistakes).

### S-8. Reading is automatic for practiced readers; naming colors is slower
- **Site says:** For someone who reads a lot, reading is automatic and happens whether you want it to or not. Naming a color is practiced much less, so it is slower and takes more effort. Practice is what makes reading automatic.
- **Source:** MacLeod (1991), above, which reviews the "relative speed of processing" and "automaticity" accounts of the Stroop effect; Stroop (1935) himself attributed the effect to practice in reading.
- **Status:** Confirmed as the standard textbook account.

## Working memory and chunking (`memory/`)

### M-1. Miller's "about seven"
- **Site says:** In 1956, psychologist George Miller described a limit of about seven items.
- **Source:** Miller, G. A. (1956). The magical number seven, plus or minus two. *Psychological Review*, 63(2), 81-97. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0043158 . Full text: https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Miller/
- **Status:** Confirmed.

### M-2. About four chunks
- **Site says:** Later research suggests the real limit is closer to about three to five chunks (often summed up as "about four") when people cannot repeat or group things.
- **Sources:**
  - Cowan, N. (2001). The magical number 4 in short-term memory. *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*, 24(1), 87-114. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X01003922
  - Cowan, N. (2010). The magical mystery four: How is working memory capacity limited, and why? *Current Directions in Psychological Science*, 19(1), 51-57. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2864034/ (open access). Central store holds about 3 to 5 meaningful items in young adults; Miller's seven was an estimate; rehearsal and grouping raise measured spans.
- **Status:** Confirmed.

### M-3. Working memory is different from long-term memory
- **Site says:** Working memory is the small workspace for what you are holding right now. Long-term memory is your huge store of things you know.
- **Source:** Cowan, N. (2008). What are the differences between long-term, short-term, and working memory? *Progress in Brain Research*, 169, 323-338. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2657600/
- **Status:** Confirmed.

### M-4. Chunks come from long-term memory
- **Site says:** A chunk is a group you already know as one thing. Chunking does not add slots; it packs more into each one, using what you already know.
- **Sources:** Cowan (2010), above. Gobet, F., et al. (2001). Chunking mechanisms in human learning. *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, 5(6), 236-243. https://doi.org/10.1016/S1364-6613(00)01662-4
- **Status:** Confirmed.

## Pattern memory and expertise (`patterns/`)

### P-1. Chase and Simon's chess memory study
- **Site says:** In the early 1970s, Chase and Simon showed chess positions to players for about five seconds. A master rebuilt real game positions far better than a beginner. When the pieces were placed randomly, the master's advantage mostly disappeared.
- **Source:** Chase, W. G., & Simon, H. A. (1973). Perception in chess. *Cognitive Psychology*, 4(1), 55-81. https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285(73)90004-2 . Builds on de Groot's 5-second recall task. On random positions all players recalled only about three or four pieces on average.
- **Status:** Confirmed.

### P-2. Masters keep a small edge on random boards
- **Site says:** Later research found that strong players still keep a small edge on random boards, so the advantage "mostly" disappears, not completely.
- **Sources:**
  - Gobet, F., & Simon, H. A. (1996). Recall of rapidly presented random chess positions is a function of skill. *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*, 3(2), 159-163. https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03212414 . Strong players "generally maintain some superiority" with random positions, but the difference is "much smaller."
  - Sala, G., & Gobet, F. (2017). Experts' memory superiority for domain-specific random material generalizes across fields of expertise: A meta-analysis. *Memory & Cognition*, 45(2), 183-193. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5323477/ (open access).
- **Status:** Corrected (the original plan said "no better"; see SPEC section 14, item 3). Note: the SPEC's "Gobet and Simon, 1996" is the *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* paper above, not their 1996 *Memory & Cognition* paper on distorted positions.

### P-3. Experts store a large library of chunks
- **Site says:** Researchers estimate that chess masters have tens of thousands of familiar chunks stored in long-term memory.
- **Source:** Gobet, F., & Simon, H. A. (1996). Recall of random and distorted chess positions: Implications for the theory of expertise. *Memory & Cognition*, 24(4), 493-503. https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03200937 . The simulation "supports the usual estimate that chess Masters store some 50,000 chunks."
- **Status:** Confirmed (site says "tens of thousands").

### P-4. The same pattern shows up in other fields
- **Site says:** The same idea shows up in music, sports, computer programming, and other fields: experts remember material from their field much better, mostly because of what they already know.
- **Source:** Sala & Gobet (2017), above. Meta-analysis covering games (chess, bridge, shogi), music, programming, sports (baseball, hockey, climbing), and other fields.
- **Status:** Confirmed. Reading X-rays appears only as a discussion question, not as a factual claim.

### P-5. Researchers study expertise by timing decisions
- **Site says:** Researchers now study expertise by timing decisions. One team looked at millions of online chess games and found that players tend to think longer in positions where extra thinking pays off, and that this link is stronger in stronger players.
- **Source:** Russek, E. M., Acosta-Kane, D., van Opheusden, B., Mattar, M. G., & Griffiths, T. L. (2025). Time spent thinking in online chess reflects the value of computation. *Cognitive Science*, 49(10), e70119. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12553403/ (open access; 12.5 million Lichess games).
- **Status:** Confirmed.

## Design and implementation facts (not shown to students)

### T-1. `event.timeStamp` is on the `performance.now()` timeline
- `Event.timeStamp` is a DOMHighResTimeStamp measured from the global object's time origin, the same origin as `performance.now()`. Widely available across browsers since 2015. Precision: Chrome 0.1 ms; Firefox and Safari round to 1 ms by default. Chrome changed from epoch time to high-resolution time in Chrome 49.
- Sources: MDN, https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Event/timeStamp ; Chrome blog, https://developer.chrome.com/blog/high-res-timestamps
- **Status:** Confirmed. 1 ms rounding is far smaller than the effects measured.

### T-2. `click` fires on release
- `click` fires after the pointer is released, so its time includes how long the finger was held. We time `pointerdown` and `keydown` instead.
- Source: UI Events specification, event order (mousedown, mouseup, click). https://www.w3.org/TR/uievents/#events-mouseevent-event-order
- **Status:** Confirmed. The SPEC's "often around 100 ms" hold time is **[VERIFY]**: no source found. It is design rationale only and never appears on the site.

### T-3. Audio onset timing
- `AudioContext.getOutputTimestamp()` returns `contextTime` (the sample frame currently being rendered by the output device) and `performanceTime` (the estimated moment that frame was rendered, on the `performance.now()` clock). Widely available since April 2021.
- Source: MDN, https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/AudioContext/getOutputTimestamp
- **Consequence:** Mapping a scheduled tone time through `getOutputTimestamp()` already accounts for the output device. Adding `outputLatency` on top would count that delay twice. So `outputLatency` is added only in the fallback path that maps through `currentTime`. See Decisions in `dev/progress.md`.

### T-4. Eric Chudler and Neuroscience for Kids
- The SPEC says Chudler retired from the University of Washington in July 2025 and said the site will continue. A search could not confirm this. The site does not repeat the claim; the Learn More page only links to Neuroscience for Kids, which was live and updated in October 2025. **[VERIFY]** not needed for site copy. Recheck the link before launch: https://faculty.washington.edu/chudler/neurok.html
