For teachers

Four short experiments where students measure their own nervous systems, then learn the neuroscience behind their numbers.

What this is

BrainLab is a free website with four experiments: reaction time, the Stroop test, working memory, and pattern memory. Each one takes 5 to 8 minutes, gives students their own results with a chart, and explains what happened in their brain using their own numbers.

  • Free. No accounts, no logins, no ads.
  • No student data. Nothing is collected, stored, or sent anywhere. Results disappear when the page is closed. Read the privacy page.
  • Built for school Chromebooks. Runs in the Chrome browser with a touchscreen, a mouse, or a keyboard. Nothing to install.
  • Sound is optional. Every experiment works with sound off. One bonus round uses a beep, and students can skip it.

A 45-minute class

  1. Hook (5 minutes). Project the Stroop test's warm-up grid and have the class call out the ink colors together. Ask: "Why was that so hard?"
  2. Experiments (20 minutes). Students run Reaction time and the Stroop test on their own or in pairs. Each takes about 5 minutes, plus 3 for the reaction time bonus round.
  3. Class data (10 minutes). Students press "Copy my results" and paste the line into your own form or spreadsheet. Compare the class's averages and spreads.
  4. Discuss (10 minutes). Use the "Talk about it" questions on each results page, or the ones in the table below.

Working memory (about 8 minutes) and Pattern memory (about 5 minutes) pair well for a second day, since the first sets up the second: experts don't have bigger working memories, they have better chunks.

Reaction time About 5 minutes, plus 3 for the bonus round The path a signal takes from eye to brain to finger, and that most of the time is spent processing in the brain. Bonus: why hearing usually beats sight. Science LS.7.1.4 (body systems, including the nervous system). Bonus round: PS.6.3.2 (light and sight) and PS.6.3.3 (sound and hearing). Stroop test About 5 minutes Automatic skills like reading run without asking permission, and the brain has to work to override them. A numbers version is available for students who find colors hard to tell apart. Science LS.7.1.4. Math: NC.7.SP.3 and NC.7.SP.4 (comparing two data sets). What else do you do automatically that would be hard to stop? Would someone just learning to read show a bigger or smaller Stroop effect? Why?
Working memory About 8 minutes Working memory is small and different from long-term memory. Chunking, using what you already know, packs more into each slot. Science LS.7.1.4. How do you remember a phone number or a locker combination? What do you know so well that it feels like one chunk?
Pattern memory About 5 minutes Experts see meaningful chunks where beginners see separate pieces. A middle school version of the classic chess memory studies. Science LS.7.1.4. What are you an expert in? Why might a doctor spot something on an X-ray in seconds that a new student would miss?

Science codes are from North Carolina's 2023 Standard Course of Study for Science. Math codes are from the North Carolina Standard Course of Study for Mathematics in use since 2018 to 2019, which is being revised. Every experiment also practices two of the science and engineering practices named in the standards: carrying out investigations, and analyzing and interpreting data.

The science practice in every experiment

Each experiment includes a controlled comparison where one thing changes: sight versus sound, matching versus mismatching words, random versus patterned digits, and random versus shaped grids. The design choices are explained to students too. The sight and sound tries alternate so practice doesn't favor one. The hidden digit patterns are mixed in with random strings. Each shaped grid has a random partner with the same number of squares. When a student's data doesn't show the expected effect, the page says so honestly and explains why that can happen.

Collecting class data

Every results page has a "Copy my results" button. It copies one plain line of text, with no name or identifier, for example:

Reaction time (sight): average 287 ms, fastest 241 ms, typical spread 23 ms, 10 trials.

Students can paste it into a form or spreadsheet that you set up. The class can then compare averages, look at the spread, and talk about why results differ. The site itself stores nothing, so you are always in control of any class data.

Accessibility

  • Every experiment works with a keyboard alone, and focus is always visible.
  • The timed parts start only when a student presses Start. Instructions and explanations are never timed.
  • Results never compare students to norms, percentiles, or each other. Every page says that these numbers don't measure how smart anyone is.
  • The Stroop test has a numbers version that doesn't depend on color vision.
  • The experiments need students to see the screen. The sound tries in the reaction time bonus round are the exception.
  • Nothing flashes more than three times per second, and animations respect the "reduce motion" setting.

Privacy in short

No accounts, cookies, browser storage, analytics, or tracking. Every page carries a security rule that blocks it from sending data anywhere. Results live only in the open browser tab. Read the full privacy page.
